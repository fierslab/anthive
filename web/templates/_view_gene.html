This panel is descriptive: for each cell type it shows how much of it expresses {{ gene }}, not whether anything changes. Click a row to list its datasets.

% expressing

The percent of cells of that cell type with at least one count of the gene, averaged over the datasets of the row (the range over datasets is in brackets). Bars run from 0 to 100 percent. In an opened row the dotted line marks the mean over all datasets of that cell type, so you see which datasets sit above or below it.

Rank in dataset

The percentile of the gene's mean (log-normalised) expression among all genes in the same dataset, shown as "top x%". A longer bar means a more highly expressed gene. The rank is only compared within one dataset, so it does not depend on how a study normalised its counts, which is why the mean expression itself is not shown.

Nuclei and cells

Detection rates are not comparable between nuclei and cells: nuclei detect long and nuclear-enriched genes better, whole cells detect cytoplasmic genes better. The tag on each dataset tells which it is, and the xenograft datasets are sorted live cells.

Datasets without per-cell cell types

Some datasets get their cell type only from a dataset-level label: they are a whole-tissue object or a mix of several cell types without a per-cell cell-type annotation. Their cells cannot be assigned to microglia, astrocytes and so on, so they are not averaged into the cell type rows. They are not clean, per-cell annotated populations. Their number is shown in one line under the table.

Low detection

A gene found in fewer than 5 percent of the cells of a cell type is called low detection (the median over the datasets of that cell type). Facet effects on such genes are fragile; panel 2 says so when it applies.

{% call section_help('2. What shifts it', 'vwg-p2-help') %}

Exploratory. A facet is a donor-level property such as sex, Braak stage or age, defined the same way in every dataset. A facet effect is a difference between donors (between host mice in the xenograft block), never between single cells, and it is tested within one cell type.

How to read the matrix

Each cell is one facet in one cell type. It shows k/m: k cohorts that are significant on their own (q below 0.05, same direction) out of m cohorts tested. Grey means not replicated. A coloured cell is replicated: the same direction in at least 2 cohorts according to a partial conjunction test at 5 percent FDR (r-value below 0.05). The darker colour means the strict test also passes: the effect is shown to exceed a minimum size, not only to differ from zero. The arrow gives the direction. A dash means not tested, because too few cohorts have the facet or the cohorts have too few replicates. A single cohort can never replicate, so it shows 0/1 or 1/1.

One significant cohort is not used as a call: in a very large cohort a good share of all genes is significant by chance, so a single hit would light up most of the matrix. Grey does not mean there is no effect: few or small cohorts cannot reach significance (p-value floor). Click a cell to see the cohorts behind it.

Systems

The post-mortem brain and the xenograft system are analysed separately and never combined (scopes). Cohorts can share a brain bank, protocol or lab, so the number of cohorts is not a count of independent confirmations. In the xenograft block all cohorts come from one lab pipeline and share iPSC lines, host strain and protocol.

Caveats shown under a matrix

  • Low detection. The gene is found in fewer than 5 percent of the cells of that cell type (median over its datasets, see panel 1). Few molecules per donor make the effect noisy and fragile.
  • Gene class. Long genes, lncRNAs, sex chromosome genes, ribosomal and mitochondrial genes are over-represented among hits for technical reasons such as nuclear RNA quality, depth or sample handling.
  • Composition. A change of a whole cell type can be a shift in the mix of cell states inside it (composition), not induction of the gene in every cell.
  • One pathology axis. Braak stage and pathologic AD are one pathology axis, so where both replicate it is one finding.
  • Age. Age rows use donors of 60 years and older. The unadjusted row re-detects part of the pathology signal; ROSMAP ages above 90 are recorded as 90, which flattens the oldest donors (age twins).
  • APOE locus. APOE, APOC1, TOMM40 and neighbours sit in the e4 locus: allele-linked expression and read-mapping differences can produce an e4 effect that need not be downstream biology.

The effect filter (at least a 10 percent change in 2 cohorts, 10 percent over three stages for Braak, per decade for age) is a separate condition on the effect size, not error control; it is listed under "More detail" when a cell is opened (effect filter).

The opened cell

The table lists one row per cohort: its size, the estimate with a 95 percent interval, and its q, the false discovery rate of that cohort on its own (a filled dot means q below 0.05). The q of a cohort is not the replication result; the r-value above combines the cohorts. Above the table the donor plot shows, per cohort, one dot per donor (per mouse in the xenograft block) with the value that was tested, so you can see the spread behind an estimate. The box "adjusted for covariates" shows the same donors after removing what the model adjusts for. Under the plot, "per-cell view in" links open a descriptive plot of the cells of an anchor dataset: it shows cells, not donors, and is not the test.
